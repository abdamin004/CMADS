\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reported the empirical results. This chapter
interprets them: how the headline outcomes fit the design intent of
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, how the memory and orchestration
comparisons should be read, what threats to validity bound the
inferences that can be drawn, and how the cost picture compares with
practical alternatives. The Limitations subsection summarises the
boundaries; the Conclusion in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} returns to
the original research question and hypothesis with these
interpretations in hand.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Interpretation of the Headline Outcomes}
\label{sec:discussion_headline}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The results show that the system can usually place the target disease,
or a closely related disease, in the differential diagnosis. The
combined found rate is 87.5\%, and the correct or related condition is
ranked first in 60.0\% of found cases (Rank-1 within found). The
pipeline also completed without any full patient-run aborts. This
supports the main design choice of the thesis: a coordinated workflow
of specialised agents is more inspectable and reliable than asking one
model to do the whole task in one call. The
Section~\ref{sec:results_single_llm} single-LLM controlled baseline
attaches a number to that claim --- the multi-agent orchestration
buys $+33.1$ percentage points on DIRECT and $+19.4$ percentage
points on Found over a single-call configuration on the same 160
patients with the same reasoning model, with the gap overwhelmingly
attributable to the orchestration ($p < 0.0001$ on 85 discordant
pairs).

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Interpretation of the Memory A/B}
\label{sec:discussion_memory}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The memory result needs careful reading. Multi-level memory did not
improve the DIRECT rate in the paired 160-patient comparison
($53.1\% \rightarrow 52.5\%$, exact McNemar $p = 1.0$ on 49 discordant
pairs). The measurable effect is on the wider differential: the Found
rate rises from 91.9\,\% to 93.8\,\% and the MISS rate falls from
8.1\,\% to 6.2\,\%, both by $1.9$ percentage points on the same
patients. The qualitative pattern in the case walkthroughs of
Section~\ref{sec:results_qualitative} is consistent with this: when
the case-based prior retrieves a clinically similar past patient, the
Diagnostic Reasoning agent more often surfaces the right disease
family in the differential, but the rubric of
Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocol} classifies cause/consequence/precursor
relations as INDIRECT, so the gain lands in Found rather than DIRECT.
This is consistent with the broader-differential interpretation: the
trade-off is recall over precision, and which direction is preferable
depends on whether the downstream consumer reads the top-1 diagnosis
or the full top-five differential.

\section{Model Comparison Read}
\label{sec:discussion_model}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The model comparison matters for practical use. On the tested
20-patient subset, GPT-OSS-120B through Groq recorded a higher
observed DIRECT point estimate than Med42-70B through local Ollama;
the Wilson 95\,\% confidence intervals on that comparison overlap at
$n = 20$, so the accuracy difference is read as exploratory rather
than as established evidence that GPT-OSS-120B is more accurate. The
latency gap is the substantive finding: GPT-OSS-120B on Groq was about
thirteen times faster than Med42-70B on local Ollama, and that gap is
several orders of magnitude larger than the noise in twenty timed
runs. In a multi-agent pipeline, every extra model call adds time, so
inference speed is not a minor implementation detail; it affects
whether the system can be used interactively.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion_threats}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Four threats to validity bound the inferences that can be drawn from
the results reported here.

\paragraph{Internal validity --- same-model coupling.}
The detectability verifier
(Section~\ref{sec:llm_verifier}) and the LLM-as-Judge evaluator
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocol}) both default to Qwen3-32B via the
shared \texttt{get\_evaluator\_llm()} factory. This means cohort
admission and outcome judging resolve to the same model, which
introduces a same-model selection/evaluation coupling on the headline
DIRECT rate. The detectability-gate sensitivity analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:results_detectability_sensitivity} is the explicit
empirical check on this coupling: re-running the pipeline on a
balanced sample drawn from the rejected confidence band of the
verifier yields 13.3\,\% DIRECT against the 73.8\,\% headline, a 60.5
percentage-point gap that is statistically unambiguous
($\chi^2 = 40.13$, $p \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-10}$). The headline is
therefore conditional on detectability, not a population-level
claim. A stronger control would have used a different LLM family for
the verifier; that is identified as future work.

\paragraph{External validity --- synthetic data only.}
All patients are synthetic and come from Synthea. The detectability
verifier selects cases where the target disease is visible in the
available data, so the evaluated cohort is cleaner than real EHR
data. Real patient records would include more missing values, noisier
histories, narrative free text, and more complex comorbidities.
External validity is therefore bounded; the results characterise the
system's behaviour on a Synthea-derived synthetic cohort with a
detectability filter, not on a representative population of real
patients.

\paragraph{Construct validity --- LLM-as-judge as sole assessor.}
Diagnostic accuracy is assessed by an LLM-as-judge protocol against
Synthea ground truth; no practising clinician reviewed the outputs as
part of the present study. The three-label DIRECT / INDIRECT / MISS
rubric, the regex-parsed five-line judge format, and the choice of a
different judge model from the reasoning model
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_protocol}) all reduce judge sensitivity to
prompt drift, but they do not substitute for clinician adjudication.
Construct validity is therefore bounded by the alignment between the
LLM judge's notion of clinical correctness and a clinician's. A
prospective clinician-in-the-loop study is the appropriate complement
and is identified as future work.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity --- non-blinded experimenter.}
The same researcher implemented the pipeline, ran the cohorts,
designed the prompts, and computed the metrics. Memory was toggled
through an environment variable rather than a blinded protocol. The
paired-McNemar design holds patient identity constant across arms,
which controls for cohort composition, but it does not control for
researcher expectation. The deck of pre-registered analyses (paired
McNemar on DIRECT, Found and MISS rates per arm,
Rank-1-within-found) was fixed before running the paired comparison;
nevertheless, the conclusion validity is bounded by the absence of an
independent replication.

\section{Token and Cost Accounting}
\label{sec:discussion_cost}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The LLM adapter does not currently persist token counts, so direct
per-patient token consumption is not reported. A first-order cost
estimate can still be derived from the persisted agent outputs and the
input case files: averaging across the 160-patient cohort, each
patient run uses an estimated 80--90~k input tokens and 18--22~k
output tokens spread across the seven agents, with the Diagnostic
Reasoning agent dominating because of its multi-round adaptive loop.
At Groq's published rates (GPT-OSS-120B at \$0.15 / M input, \$0.75 /
M output; Qwen3-32B at \$0.29 / M input, \$0.59 / M output) this
gives an estimated cost of \textbf{approximately \$0.03 per patient
run} (reasonable range \$0.01--\$0.04 depending on diagnostic-round
count and treatment-planning eligibility). At this rate, the
160-patient cohort cost approximately \$5; a 1{,}000-patient
evaluation would cost approximately \$30. The estimate excludes
embedding and Qdrant infrastructure costs (both negligible at this
scale) and is conservative on the input side because the Diagnostic
Reasoning loop's intermediate-call inputs are not separately
persisted. Direct token instrumentation in the LLM adapter is
identified as future work; once added, the estimate above can be
replaced with measured numbers per patient.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limits}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The results have clear limits. All patients are synthetic and come
from Synthea. The detectability verifier selects cases where the
target disease is visible in the available data, so the cohort is
cleaner than real EHR data. Real patient records would include more
missing values, noisier histories, and more complex comorbidities.
Some disease families, especially type-2 diabetes and chronic
congestive heart failure, have small counts, so their per-family
percentages should not be overinterpreted. The memory comparison is
also limited in size and should be repeated on a larger and more
balanced cohort. Finally, this is a research prototype, not a
deployable medical device. The dashboard is intended to support
review by a clinician, and the clinician remains the decision maker.
