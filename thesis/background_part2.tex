\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The 22 selected papers are grouped into five themes. The first group covers early
multi-agent medical reasoning systems. The second group focuses on agent
coordination and workflow design. The third group studies debate, disagreement,
and conflict resolution between agents. The fourth group reviews systems built
for specific clinical domains. The final group covers safety, reliability, and
evaluation.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Foundational Multi-Agent Frameworks for Clinical AI}
\label{subsec:foundational}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Four early systems shaped the field. They introduced role-based agents,
discussion loops, final synthesis, and supervisor-style review. These ideas are
reused in many later systems, including the system built in this thesis.
Table~\ref{tab:tracker_foundational} summarises the papers before the discussion.

MedAgents by Tang et al. is one of the main starting points for this area
\cite{Tang2024MedAgents}. It uses role-assigned medical agents that first analyse
a query separately, then discuss it, and finally produce a shared answer. It was
tested on nine medical benchmarks, including MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and MMLU
medical tasks. The system performed better than the baselines in a zero-shot
setting, and the discussion step was the most useful part in the ablation study.
Its error analysis is also important for this thesis: 77\% of the reviewed LLM
medical errors were linked to knowledge gaps. The main limitation is that the
evaluation was still based on benchmark questions, not a full clinical workflow
with patient records, memory, or treatment planning.

openCHA by Abbasian et al. adds a stronger connection to external data and tools
\cite{Abbasian2024openCHA}. It uses an interface layer, an orchestrator, and
external sources. The orchestrator plans tasks, executes them, stores
intermediate results, builds prompts, and generates the final response. The
system was shown through two live demonstrations and four use cases, including
dietary guidance and diabetes management. For this thesis, the useful part is
the separation between planning and execution. However, openCHA does not focus on
parallel clinical agents or structured disagreement between agents.

AI Hospital by Fan et al. studies what happens when agents interact over several
turns \cite{Fan2024AIHospital}. It includes a Doctor agent, Patient agent,
Examiner agent, and Chief Physician agent. The Patient reveals symptoms over
time, while the Doctor asks questions and requests tests. The result showed that
multi-turn diagnosis is much harder than answering with all information given at
once. It also showed that removing dispute resolution lowered diagnostic
accuracy. This supports the Reviewer-Refiner pattern used in this thesis.

The MAC framework by Shen et al. models diagnosis as a group consultation
\cite{MAC2025npj}. Doctor agents discuss, critique, and refine their answers
under a Supervisor agent. On 302 rare-disease cases, the best setup was four
Doctor agents and one Supervisor. Adding too many agents made the discussion
redundant. MAC also performed better than Self-Consistency, even though
Self-Consistency used more model calls. This is useful evidence that organised
discussion can be better than simply sampling many independent answers.

\subsection{Agent Coordination Architectures and Workflow Orchestration}
\label{subsec:coordination}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The first group of papers shows the value of multiple agents, but many of those
systems use a fixed structure for every case. The next group focuses on how
agents should be coordinated. This includes adaptive routing, escalation,
workflow decomposition, and dynamic loops. Table~\ref{tab:tracker_coordination}
summarises the papers in this group.

MDAgents by Kim et al. is important because it does not use the same agent setup
for every query \cite{Kim2024MDAgents}. It first classifies the case as low,
moderate, or high complexity. Low-complexity cases can be handled by one agent,
while harder cases use a group or a multidisciplinary team. Across ten medical
benchmarks, MDAgents achieved the best result on seven. The adaptive setup also
performed better than fixed solo and fixed group settings by up to 4.2\%, and a
Moderator agent with retrieval improved group accuracy further. The trade-off was
latency: high-complexity cases took much longer than low-complexity cases.

TAO, also by Kim et al., applies a similar idea to safety \cite{Kim2025TAO}.
Instead of only routing by difficulty, it routes by risk. Tier~1 handles routine
low-risk work, Tier~2 uses specialist agents for moderate cases, and Tier~3 is
used for high-risk decisions. An Agent Recruiter chooses the starting tier, while
an Agent Router can escalate a case when uncertainty appears. The result is
useful for this thesis because it shows that orchestration can be designed around
safety, not only accuracy.

Bedi et al. give one of the most relevant warnings for this thesis
\cite{Bedi2025OptParadox}. Their Optimisation Paradox study compared three
systems on 2{,}400 real MIMIC-CDM patient cases: a single agent, a coordinated
homogeneous multi-agent system, and a Best-of-Breed system that used the strongest
individual component for each stage. The Best-of-Breed system performed worse,
with 67.7\% diagnostic accuracy and a 13.87\% test-result hallucination rate.
This shows that strong parts do not automatically make a strong system. The
handoffs and coordination between agents can be more important than the isolated
quality of each agent. Figure~\ref{fig:opt_paradox} shows the workflow used in
that study.

Barra et al. provide a practical workflow example rather than only a benchmark
study \cite{Barra2025Agentic}. Their n8n pipeline uses seven agents and combines
task decomposition, prompt chaining, parallel work, retrieval, and refinement. A
Reviewer Agent checks the output against INACSL and ASPiH standards before an
Editor Agent formats it. Across 50 runs, the pipeline reduced development time
by 70--80\%. The main idea used in this thesis is the separation between
generation, review, and final formatting.

DynamiCare by Shang et al. focuses on multi-round clinical interaction
\cite{Shang2025DynamiCare}. Instead of giving the full case to the agents at the
start, the system has to ask a Patient Agent for more information over time. This
is closer to a real consultation, where the doctor gathers history, examination
findings, and investigations step by step. The paper also introduced the
MIMIC-Patient benchmark for dynamic agent-based clinical decision-making.

\subsection{Inter-Agent Deliberation, Debate, and Conflict Resolution}
\label{subsec:deliberation}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Coordination decides which agents run and in what order. A separate question is
what the system should do when agents disagree. The papers in this group study
debate, dissent, and bias reduction. They are important because a clinical MAS
should not simply accept the first answer without challenge.

MDTeamGPT by Chen et al. models a multidisciplinary team consultation
\cite{Chen2025MDTeamGPT}. The workflow has four phases: assign roles, let agents
analyse independently, debate, and synthesise the final answer. On MedQA and
MedMCQA, it performed better than single-agent and fixed-team baselines. The
debate phase was the most useful part. The paper also found that performance was
better when agents started with different initial diagnoses. This supports the
idea that disagreement can be useful when it is structured.

Layered Debate by Zhao et al. focuses on diseases that look similar
\cite{Zhao2025LayeredDebate}. The first layer asks specialist agents to produce
initial diagnoses. The second layer makes them cross-examine each other. The
third layer uses a Judge Agent to read the debate and make the final decision.
The system performed better than single-agent, standard multi-agent, and
chain-of-thought baselines. For this thesis, the useful idea is that review
should examine the reasoning path, not only the final diagnosis.

Wang et al. identify a common failure called \textit{silent agreement}
\cite{Wang2025Catfish}. This happens when agents appear to discuss a case but
quickly agree with the first diagnosis without real criticism. Their Catfish
Agent is designed to inject controlled dissent into the discussion. It changes
the strength of the disagreement based on case difficulty and keeps the tone
collaborative enough to avoid harming performance. Across twelve benchmarks, it
outperformed strong baselines, especially on difficult cases. This supports the
need for a reviewer agent that can challenge the diagnostic agent.

Ke et al. connect disagreement to safety \cite{Ke2024CognitiveBias}. They tested
whether multi-agent LLM conversations can reduce anchoring, availability, and
confirmation bias. Their three-role setup used a primary decision agent, expert
consultants, and a critical evaluator. Across ten clinical vignettes, the
multi-agent setting produced fewer bias events and better diagnostic accuracy.
The critical evaluator was the most useful part because it stopped the primary
agent from staying with a wrong early diagnosis when later evidence contradicted
it.
