\section{Concepts Overview}
\label{sec:concepts}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter gives the background needed to understand the system built in this
thesis. It first explains why a single LLM is not enough for clinical decision
support, then introduces multi-agent systems, clinical LLM agents, retrieval, and
workflow design. The second half of the chapter reviews the papers that shaped
the implementation choices used later in Chapter~3.

\FloatBarrier
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Limitations of Single-Model AI in Clinical Settings}
\label{subsec:single_model_limits}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Single LLM systems have been used for diagnosis support, drug recommendation, and
clinical documentation. They can produce useful medical text and often perform
well on question-answering benchmarks \cite{Abbasian2024openCHA}. However, a
clinical workflow is harder than a benchmark. The model has to work with partial
evidence, uncertainty, and information that may be spread across many parts of a
patient record.

The first problem is missing knowledge. A model only has the knowledge learned
during training unless it is connected to external data or tools. It cannot
automatically query a patient database, check a current guideline, or calculate a
clinical score. Tang et al. found that 77\% of LLM medical-task errors were
linked to domain knowledge gaps rather than pure reasoning failure
\cite{Tang2024MedAgents}. This supports the idea of using specialist agents and
retrieval, instead of asking one model to carry the whole task.

The second problem is single-pass reasoning. A normal LLM call produces one
answer from one prompt. Clinical reasoning often needs several rounds: collect
evidence, form a differential, check missing information, revise the answer, and
only then decide. Shang et al. showed that dynamic multi-round decision-making
performed better than single-pass approaches on complex patient cases
\cite{Shang2025DynamiCare}.

The third problem is bias. LLMs can anchor on an early diagnosis, look mainly for
evidence that supports it, or rely on familiar patterns. These are similar to
known diagnostic biases in human medicine. Ke et al. showed that multi-agent
conversation with a critical evaluator reduced anchoring, availability, and
confirmation bias compared with a single-agent setting \cite{Ke2024CognitiveBias}.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Systems: Principles and Architecture}
\label{subsec:mas_principles}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A multi-agent system (MAS) divides a task between several agents. In clinical AI,
each agent usually has a role, such as retrieving information, reading the EHR,
interpreting labs, reasoning about diagnoses, or reviewing the answer
\cite{Kim2024MDAgents}. The agents exchange structured messages and can use
different prompts, tools, or knowledge sources.

The way agents are connected matters. In a centralised design, one orchestrator
assigns work and combines the outputs. In a decentralised design, agents exchange
messages more directly. In a hierarchical design, simple cases can be handled at
a lower level and difficult or risky cases can be escalated. Chen et al. showed
that topology affects safety: centralised systems were more vulnerable to a
compromised agent, while decentralised systems were more resilient
\cite{Chen2025MedSentry}. Kim et al. also used a tiered design in TAO to improve
safety handling \cite{Kim2025TAO}.

Specialisation is another important idea. A clinical MAS should not use several
copies of the same general agent and expect them to add much value. The stronger
pattern is to give agents different responsibilities, such as a Retrieval Agent,
Reasoning Agent, Synthesis Agent, or Critical Evaluator. This pattern appears in
many of the systems reviewed in this chapter
\cite{Zhou2024ZODIAC,Yan2024ClinicalLab}.

\subsection{Large Language Models as Clinical Agents}
\label{subsec:llm_agents}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

In this thesis, an LLM agent is a model call wrapped in a defined role, a prompt,
an input format, and an output schema. The LLM provides the language reasoning,
but the surrounding workflow controls what evidence the model sees and how its
answer is checked.

Different LLMs vary in size, training data, instruction following, and medical
accuracy. The literature shows that the largest model is not always the best
choice if the workflow is weak. Chen et al. reported that a Llama-3.1-70B
multi-agent system outperformed GPT-4o on rare-disease diagnosis when the smaller
model was supported by memory and tools \cite{Chen2024RareAgents}. This is
important for this thesis because it shifts attention from only choosing a model
to designing the workflow around the model.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is one way to reduce missing knowledge. With
RAG, an agent receives retrieved documents, such as clinical guidelines, before
it writes its answer. Choi et al. showed that adding RAG to a multi-agent
pharmacovigilance system reduced hallucinated drug-event associations by 64\%
\cite{Choi2024MALADE}. This motivates the guideline retrieval used later by the
Treatment Planning agent.

\subsection{Automation Workflow Design in Clinical AI}
\label{subsec:workflow_design}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Automation workflow design means breaking a clinical task into clear steps and
deciding which agent or tool handles each step. It also defines how outputs move
between agents and where quality checks are placed \cite{Barra2025Agentic}.

Most clinical MAS workflows follow a simple pattern: gather evidence, analyse it,
then produce a final answer. Later systems extend this pattern with parallel
agent work, adaptive loops, retrieval, review, and error recovery
\cite{Tang2024MedAgents,Barra2025Agentic,Lu2024TriageAgent,Wu2024EHRFlow}.
Bedi et al. add an important warning: better individual agents do not guarantee a
better system if the handoffs between agents are poor \cite{Bedi2025OptParadox}.

For this thesis, the workflow must handle four practical needs. It must divide
the clinical task into agent roles, coordinate the order of execution, allow
agents to challenge each other, and prevent one weak output from silently
corrupting later stages. The literature review below explains how earlier work
handled these needs and where gaps remain.

\subsection{Clinical Reasoning and Decision-Making}
\label{subsec:clinical_reasoning}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The systems reviewed in this chapter all attempt to support a process that
clinicians normally carry out themselves. A short primer on that process helps
to read the rest of the chapter, especially for readers from a computing
background.

A \emph{differential diagnosis} is a ranked list of conditions that could
plausibly explain a patient's presentation. Clinicians build it by combining
the patient's history, current symptoms, vital signs, prior conditions,
medications, and laboratory and imaging findings, and they update it as new
information arrives. The ranking is not just a list of possibilities; each
candidate carries an implicit probability and an implicit cost of missing it,
and treatment decisions are made against the top-ranked candidate while
remaining alert to the alternatives. A clinical decision support (CDS) system
is any tool intended to help with this process, from rule-based alerts in an
electronic health record through to the multi-agent LLM systems reviewed
below.

Clinical reasoning is well documented to be cognitive-bias-prone. Three biases
matter most in the literature reviewed here. \emph{Anchoring} occurs when an
early hypothesis disproportionately influences later reasoning: a clinician
who suspects pulmonary embolism early may interpret a borderline D-dimer as
supportive even when the broader history points elsewhere.
\emph{Availability bias} occurs when recently seen or memorable cases are
weighted too heavily: a doctor who recently diagnosed a rare condition may
over-suspect it in the next patient. \emph{Confirmation bias} is the tendency
to look for evidence that supports an initial hypothesis and to discount
evidence that contradicts it. Ke et al.\ show that LLM agents reproduce these
biases when used as single models and that structured multi-agent
configurations can attenuate them~\cite{Ke2024CognitiveBias}.

Two further properties of clinical reasoning shape the design of any CDS
system. First, the evidence is \emph{longitudinal}: laboratory trends over
months matter, not just the most recent value. Second, the reasoning is
\emph{multi-source}: a single answer typically rests on data from the history,
the examination, the chart, and any retrieved guideline or reference. A CDS
system that processes only the current visit, or only one type of evidence,
will miss precisely the cases where reasoning is hardest. The pipeline built
in this thesis is designed to combine these sources explicitly, and the
review below selects work that does the same.

\section{Search Strategy}
\label{sec:search}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature search focused on recent multi-agent and agentic LLM work in
clinical AI. Searches were conducted between January and May 2026 in arXiv,
PubMed, ACL Anthology, IEEE Xplore, and Semantic Scholar. The main search
terms were ``multi-agent system AND clinical decision'', ``LLM agent AND
medical reasoning'', ``automation workflow AND clinical AI'', ``multi-agent
AND electronic health records'', and ``agent collaboration AND healthcare''.
The five searches together returned approximately 410 candidate articles
across the databases, with substantial overlap between sources.

The candidate set was filtered in three stages. First, duplicate records and
non-research items (editorials, course notes, blog posts) were removed,
reducing the set to about 180 distinct articles. Second, titles and abstracts
were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria of
Table~\ref{tab:inclusion}; this left approximately 55 articles for full-text
review. Third, full-text review removed papers that did not propose or
evaluate a working multi-agent system, that focused only on medical image
analysis without a clinical decision component, or that were review articles
without an original empirical contribution. After this process, 22 papers
were retained for the multi-agent clinical AI literature review and
grouped into the five themes used below. The wider thesis bibliography
additionally cites the CoALA cognitive-architecture taxonomy, the
BioLORD embedding model used by the case-based memory and retrieval
layers, the epidemiology / EHR-workload sources cited in
Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}, and the AMIE and AgentClinic
contemporary works discussed below as the closest published evaluation
shapes to this thesis's protocol; these sit outside the MAS review
count and bring the total bibliography to 29 entries.

A paper was included when it proposed or evaluated a multi-agent or agentic
LLM system, applied it to a medical task, and reported either quantitative
results or clinician-reviewed outcomes. Single-model systems, image-only
systems without clinical decision support, and review papers without new
experiments were excluded. Table~\ref{tab:inclusion} summarises the criteria.
